Sixty storms

One thing changed at a time from the usual setting (50 bids a second, holds taking 150 to 500 ms, 3 cards in 100 declined), five runs at each value, on one laptop.

What came out

  • Slower card holds, fewer accepted bids. About 110 with an instant answer, about 65 at 50 to 150 ms, about 35 to 40 at the usual setting and about 18 at 500 to 1500 ms. Runs at each value stayed fairly close together.
  • Request rate mattered too, more than guessed. The guess written down before running was that hold delay would matter much more than rate. From 10 to 50 bids a second, accepted bids rose from about 20 to about 35 to 40. At 100, runs ranged from about 20 to 45. At 200 and 300, replies took seconds and fewer than 15 bids were accepted.
  • Declined cards made less difference. The middle drifted from about 40 accepted with none declined to about 30 with half declined, but the runs overlap.
  • Every valid run passed every check. That is 58 of 60. The other two are invalid (one skipped sends at 300 a second, one sent a bid late) and are drawn hollow and left out of the middle values.

What this doesn’t show

  • Not the target’s capacity. The generator, two target instances, the payment fake, the database and the subscribers shared one busy 8-core laptop (load average up to about 15), so the slowdown at high rates is the laptop’s as much as anything.
  • Accepted counts depend on how these bidders behave: they read the price every tenth of a second and bid just above it, in an 8-second auction. Real bidders needn’t.
  • Typical reply time mixes bids that needed a card hold with bids refused without one, so it can jump between runs. The slow reply time is steadier.
  • Five runs a value is a small sample, and nothing here tests two variables changing together.
  • Nothing here that you can’t check: the sixty raw recordings (about 4 MB, xz) are the source of every mark.

Every run is a mark; the bar is the middle of the valid ones. Hollow marks are invalid runs. Values along the bottom are evenly spaced, not to scale.
